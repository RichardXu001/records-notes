What a Receiving Record Should Capture

A receiving record is written during the one hour when nobody yet knows what the shipment will turn out to be, which is precisely why it earns its place. A week later the carton is gone, the outer packaging has been binned, the material has been split into working portions, and every question about arrival condition has to be answered from whatever someone wrote down while the courier was still standing at the bench. The record is not paperwork wrapped around the material. It is the part of the material that survives handling.

Time Comes First

Every other entry borrows its meaning from a timestamp. A condition note without a time is an opinion; the same note with a time is evidence. Write the arrival time to the minute, and write a second time for when the outer packaging was opened, because the gap between those two numbers is the window an unopened box spent in a corridor, on a dock, or beside a warm loading door. If the material went into storage before anyone opened the box, say so on the same line. The point is that a reader a year from now can rebuild the sequence, and a sequence with no times cannot be rebuilt.

Five Fields That Earn Their Space

Most of what a receiving record needs fits into five entries. The name of the person recording is less a sixth field than a condition of the other five, and it belongs on the same line as the time rather than in a signature box at the foot of the sheet.

  • Arrival date and time to the minute, followed by the time the packaging was opened.
  • Outer packaging condition in observable terms: crushed corner, damp carton, tape intact, cold pack still solid, label legible or not.
  • Lot number exactly as printed, with the supplier name and catalogue reference written beside it rather than inferred from it.
  • Quantity in the unit the label uses, plus a few words on how the figure was reached — counted, weighed, or carried over from a secondary container.
  • Storage location written as an address: unit, shelf, position, and the container the material actually went into.

Notice what the list avoids: any verdict. Condition is written as a description because a verdict at the door closes a question before anyone has had the chance to look at it. The same discipline applies to quantity. Writing 4.8 g beside a stated 5 g is useful; writing 4.8 g without noting whether the balance had been zeroed, or whether the vial was weighed with its cap on, is not, and the difference surfaces months later when someone tries to reconcile the figure.

A Photograph Records a Camera

A phone photograph feels like a complete record because it captures so much at once, and it is the most common stand-in for a written line. A picture of a carton carries no scale, no name attached to it, no interpretation, and a filename that loses its meaning the first time it is copied into a shared folder. Photographs still have a place, because they show what a description struggles to reach: the shape of a crushed corner, or the way a cold pack sat around a secondary container. Keep them as attachments. The written line is the record and the image waits behind it.

Field Order Is an Instruction

Order tells the person filling the form what the form is for. Begin with quantity and the implicit message is that counting is the task; begin with time and the message is that sequence is the task. Two placements carry more weight than the rest. Condition should come before identity, since a damaged carton changes how a printed label ought to be read, and smudged print on a dry box means something different from smudged print on a damp one. Storage belongs last, because writing a location is an act of acceptance, and acceptance should close the exercise rather than open it. A form built in another order still works, and it is worth reading a worked checklist for batch records before fixing your own sequence.

What the Sheet Should Not Carry

Three things creep into receiving sheets and all three belong somewhere else. The first is a conclusion, written as a pass or a fail, which pre-empts a review that has not happened yet. The second is an adjective with nothing observable behind it, such as acceptable or poor, which a second person cannot check. The third is a plan, phrased as though the sheet were a to-do list: reorder, quarantine, write to the supplier. Plans change and the record should not. Where a decision was made at the bench, put the decision and its reason in a separate note, and leave the receiving entry as a description of what arrived.

The test is easy to apply. Hand the sheet to a colleague who was not present and ask what the parcel looked like at half past nine on the morning it arrived. If the answer comes back as a reconstruction rather than a guess, the page did its job. If it comes back as a shrug, no amount of photographing will repair it, and the next delivery is the one to work on.
