Note 02 · Identification

Anatomy of a Lot Number

A lot number is a name, not a description. It identifies a group of material that a supplier made or packed together, and it exists so that two people can point at the same group without arguing about which bottle they mean. That is a narrower job than most readers assume, and the distance between the job and the assumption is where confusion about lots begins.

What the String Is Doing

The string on the label has one primary duty: it must be reproducible. Someone at the supplier's bench assigns it, someone types it into a system, and from that moment it has to survive handwriting, transcription, a decade of filing, and at least one change of software. Everything else the number appears to say — when the material was filled, which line did the work, which project it belonged to — is a side effect of how one supplier chose to build its identifiers. Side effects can be useful. They are never promises.

What It Can Encode

Four fragments show up again and again, in different orders and different disguises.

  • A date fragment, often the year and month of filling, sometimes in a form that only becomes readable once you have seen two examples side by side.
  • A sequence number, which fixes the order of filling within a run and says nothing about the material itself.
  • A site or line code, useful when a supplier fills in more than one place and the two places keep separate paperwork.
  • A project or customer prefix, added when material is packed to a particular arrangement rather than to general stock.

Read together, the fragments describe a manufacturing event. They do not describe material.

What It Cannot Encode

A lot number cannot say what is inside. Specifications live in a separate document, and the number is only the pointer to it. It cannot encode an expiry as a property of the material, because expiry is assigned against a stated storage condition and the condition is not in the string. It cannot carry chain of custody, since the number stays identical through every hand that touches the vial, every repack, and every move between units. Nor can it establish that two containers are interchangeable, even when the numbers sit next to each other on one invoice; adjacency in a numbering scheme is an accident of production, not a statement of equivalence.

Three Suppliers, Three Habits

Numbering systems diverge in ways that surprise anyone who has only seen one. A common pattern begins with the date: eight digits for year, month and day, a short sequence, and a letter for the filling line. Another begins with the sequence, a low number that increments forever with no date anywhere inside it. A third is close to opaque, a project code plus a four-character internal reference that means something only inside the supplier's own system. None of these is better than the others. What matters is that your record does not depend on decoding the string, because decoding becomes guesswork the moment a convention changes without announcement, which it does.

One String, Three Systems

The lot string earns its place by staying identical in three separate places.

  1. In the record. Every receiving entry, storage movement and aliquot note carries the string verbatim, copied from the label rather than from memory or from the invoice, because the invoice and the label disagree more often than anyone expects.
  2. On the label. Once material is split, a working container needs the string plus a split date and an owner; without it, a vial becomes anonymous the first time its parent container leaves the shelf.
  3. In the ledger. An inventory export sorted by lot string answers a different question from one sorted by date, and it answers the question people actually ask after an incident: where else did this number go?

The three uses only hold together if the string is copied without damage. Hyphens turn into dashes when text passes through a word processor, a capital O becomes a zero because someone retyped it from a photograph, and a trailing letter drops off when a column is too narrow.

Copying the string deserves a convention of its own: characters exactly as printed, separators preserved, no spaces inserted. Where the string is long, break the line at the supplier's own separators rather than at your own margin. And when the label is the only original, photograph it beside the receiving entry and keep the image with the record, since the image is the version a later reader can check against.

Used as a key, the lot number behaves. Asked to be a description, it will fail at the worst possible moment. The working rule fits in a sentence: copy what is printed, point to the document that describes the material, and let the number do the one job it was built for.
