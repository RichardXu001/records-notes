Storage Log Fields That Actually Get Used

Most storage logs fail quietly. Nobody rejects them, nobody rewrites them, and the rows keep accumulating for years while the only question anyone asks — what happened to this material between March and June — stays unanswerable from the page. The columns that matter are fewer than most templates assume, and the ones that get filled in reliably are fewer still.

Columns That Survive a Busy Week

The test of a column is not whether it would be useful. It is whether someone will still complete it on a Friday afternoon with a queue at the door and a courier waiting for a signature.

  • The reading and the moment it was taken, with the interval between readings written once for the period rather than repeated on every line.
  • The location as an address — unit identifier, shelf, position — so a reading can be tied to a physical place rather than to a nickname someone invented years ago.
  • The value and its unit together, written as a number and a symbol, never as a word such as cold.
  • The method: a logger export, a handheld probe, or a dial read by eye through a door.
  • The person who took or retrieved the reading, and the file name of the raw export when there is one.

Two of those columns do most of the work. Without a time, a reading is a rumour. Without a location, a reading is a rumour about a room.

Instrument and Hand, Side by Side

Logger and manual entries are not rivals; they answer different questions. A logger supplies density, a value every few minutes, including the hours when the building is empty. A handwritten entry supplies context: who opened the door, when the delivery arrived, why the probe was moved. What neither supplies is interpretation, and interpretation belongs in a dated note rather than in the margin of a column. Plenty of teams run both and then trust only the export, which throws away the one thing the handwriting was good for. The labs that end up with a log that survives a busy week usually get there by cutting columns and naming an owner for each page.

A shared page needs a handover habit more than it needs another column. A reading written at the door is an observation; a reading reconstructed at the end of a shift is a summary of what someone remembers, and the two look identical once both are ink. Where a page is used across shifts, the line worth protecting is the handover note, because it is the only place where a person can record that the door stood ajar during a delivery, or that the probe was pushed aside to make room for a box.

After a Reading Leaves the Range

The order of the questions matters more than the speed of the answer.

  1. Confirm the reading before believing it: where the probe sits, whether a door stood open during a delivery, whether the unit defrosts on a schedule, whether a sensor was moved by someone stacking boxes.
  2. Establish the duration. One reading out of range at the end of a week says little; two consecutive readings bracket a window, and a gap between them is a finding in its own right.
  3. Set the boundary from the material's own documentation — the condition it was assigned and how long it may sit outside it — rather than from how bad the number looks.
  4. Record the disposition with a name and a date, even when the disposition is that nothing changes.

Each step can settle the question before the next one is reached, and that is the reason for the order. A log that skips straight to the fourth step produces a decision with no traceable reason behind it, which is exactly the kind of entry that gets reopened two years later.

How Long the Record Stays

Retention is set by the material, not by the calendar year. The practical rule is the life of the material plus a margin long enough to cover a question raised after the last container is gone, and longer wherever the log is the only evidence of the condition a shipment experienced. Keep the raw export, not just a monthly summary typed from it; the summary is an interpretation and the export is the reading. Keep the handwritten pages too, scanned if the paper cannot stay. A log discarded at the end of the financial year usually disappears a few months before the one enquiry that needed it.

None of this requires a new system. It requires a page with five columns, a name against each one, and the discipline to write the time before writing the temperature. The log that gets read later is almost never the most detailed one. It is the one that was finished.
