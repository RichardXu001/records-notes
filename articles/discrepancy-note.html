Note 04 · Discrepancies

Writing a Discrepancy Note Nobody Argues With

A discrepancy note is read by two audiences, and only one of them was there. The person who wrote it remembers the carton, the light in the room, the balance, and the conversation at the bench; the person who reads it six months later has the page and nothing else. Notes that survive review are written for the second reader, and they are built in a shape that makes disagreement difficult.

Separate What Was Seen From What Was Thought

Observation, measurement and inference are three different things, and they belong in three different sentences. The carton arrived with a crushed corner is an observation. The secondary container weighed 4.6 g against a stated 5 g is a measurement. The material was probably exposed to a warm room is an inference, and it needs the word probably or it stops being honest. A figure without its source is only slightly better than an impression, which is why the second half of any measurement is the document it came from. Most disputed notes have collapsed at least two of the three into a single line, usually the one that reads best.

The Three-Paragraph Shape

Almost every note that ends a conversation can be built from three paragraphs in a fixed order.

  1. Fact. What was compared with what, both values, both sources, and the time. No adjectives and no causes.
  2. Impact. What the difference prevents, limits or leaves uncertain, expressed as what can and cannot be done next rather than as how serious it feels.
  3. Disposition. What was decided, by whom, on what date, and what remains open. If nothing was decided, say so and give the date by which it will be.

The order is doing real work. A reader who meets the fact first can form an opinion; a reader who meets the disposition first reads everything afterwards as justification and starts looking for the gap in it.

Length is not the measure. A note that runs to a page without ever stating what was compared with what has failed in its first paragraph, while three short paragraphs holding the right numbers can settle a question in a single reading.

The Sentences That Start Arguments

What gets argued about is rarely the measurement. It is the sentence that assigns a reason. A note saying the courier mishandled the parcel invites a dispute about the courier; a note saying the carton arrived crushed and the cold pack was liquid invites a review of the packing. Fault is the most common contaminant in a discrepancy note, and it is usually invisible to the person writing it, because fault feels like explanation. One useful check is to read each sentence and ask whether a second person holding the same evidence could disagree. If they could disagree about the wording rather than the facts, the wording is carrying too much.

What Does Not Belong

Four kinds of sentence cause most of the trouble. Judgments about a supplier's competence belong in a review meeting, not in a record that follows the material for years. Adjectives such as serious or unacceptable carry an argument inside them and cannot be verified by anyone. Speculation about cause should be labelled as speculation or left out until there is evidence. Promises and dates that nobody agreed to will be quoted back later as though they were commitments. Anything written in this note may be read by someone who was not in the room, so write only what that reader could confirm from the same evidence.

Write It as Though It Will Be Disputed

Assume a reader who wants a different conclusion and give them everything needed to reach it. Quote both values instead of summarising the difference, name the document each figure came from, and keep the timeline in sequence even where the sequence is unflattering. A note written this way is harder to attack not because it is defensive but because it is complete, and completeness is the only protection that keeps working after the author has left the building. It also means dating every entry, including the ones added late, since a note assembled from memory the following week is still useful as long as the reader can see when it was assembled.

The measure of a good discrepancy note is simple. If the reader can restate the problem, the impact and the decision without asking a question, the note is finished. If they have to ask who decided, or when, or against which figure, the next version has one more paragraph to write.
